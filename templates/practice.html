An AI Lab Buddy That Guides Troubleshooting Instead of Giving Answers

Ahmet Can Sabuncu
Assistant Professor of Teaching, Mechanical & Materials Engineering
Faculty page →

Sahil Mirani
Robotics Engineering & Mechanical and Materials Engineering, WPI Class of 2026

Engineering Research 28 min

Since 2024, Sahil Mirani has been building an AI "troubleshooting buddy" with Prof. Sabuncu for ME3901/ME3902 (Engineering Experimentation), where students build electromechanical hardware and debug it with code. The motivation: commercial chatbots tend to converge fast on one diagnosis and hand over the fix, which invites cognitive offloading and short-circuits exactly the troubleshooting skill the course is trying to build.
The tool is a multi-agent pipeline, built no-code in a drag-and-drop, LangChain-style platform (Flowise, now being replaced after it was sunset) using natural-language instructions rather than code. Students can type, talk, or upload files and photos of their circuit. For domain-knowledge questions ("how does this component work?") it answers directly; for troubleshooting questions, it uses Socratic-style questioning and quick verification steps — including simple ASCII circuit diagrams — to help students explore several hypotheses before narrowing in, rather than announcing the fix.
Nothing in the current flow is course-specific: adapting it to another class means adding one node with your own lecture slides, assignments, lab documents, or even just a short markdown file of course context — no programming required. Two earlier lab versions were piloted with students and revised from feedback; the current version hasn't been classroom-tested yet. Can is candid about the rough edges: Socratic-style prompting can misfire on a simple factual question ("what's atmospheric pressure at sea level?" deserves an answer, not "what do you think it is?"), and packing roughly 50,000 characters of custom instructions into one system prompt starts to produce contradictions. Estimated running cost: about 1–2 cents per message, or roughly $100 per term for a 30-student course.

What you can try: If you're building or adapting an AI tutor for troubleshooting-style work, explicitly instruct it to resist converging on one diagnosis — have it help students weigh a few hypotheses before narrowing down — and separate "just tell me a fact" questions from "guide me through a problem" questions. You don't need to build this from scratch: Sahil and Prof. Sabuncu's framework is intentionally course-agnostic, so pointing it at your own lecture notes or a short markdown file of course context is enough to try it in your own class.

Building a Custom AI-Writing Detection Tool for Homework

Narahara Chari Dingari
Adjunct Teaching Professor, Data Science & Computer Science
Faculty page →

Data Science Teaching 12 min

Chari built his own AI-writing detection tool for grading homework in his data science, big data analytics, and generative AI courses. Rather than a yes/no verdict, the tool returns a score built from multiple signals researchers use to characterize machine-generated text — vocabulary density, perplexity, sentence-length variation, monotonous rhythm, uniformly impersonal voice, telltale punctuation like em-dashes, and stock transition phrases. He uploads submissions to the tool by hand; it isn't connected to Canvas.
A score above roughly 70% is treated as most likely AI-generated, and the student is asked to redo the work rather than penalized outright. He draws a clear line between using AI to learn a concept and using it to generate the submitted work, and says that distinction has held up well with students — he's had only one pushback, early on. He's aware of third-party detectors like GPTZero and Grammarly's built-in detection, but built his own so he can pull in findings from recent research as the field moves; he expects to retune the metrics occasionally, though some (like perplexity) have stayed stable.
Because his courses center on code, data, and generated reports rather than extended prose, he hasn't consulted with WPI's writing faculty on the tool — he sees it as suited to technical, data-heavy coursework rather than writing-intensive courses.

What you can try: If you're evaluating AI-detection tools (built your own or using one like GPTZero), consider a graduated score rather than a binary flag, set an explicit threshold for follow-up, and give students a chance to redo the work instead of an automatic penalty. Be explicit with students about the difference between using AI to learn a concept versus using it to produce the submission.

Using Copilot to Work Through Everyday Excel Problems

Tobie Morris
Operations Manager
Department of Mechanical & Materials Engineering
WPI staff page →

Engineering Admin 7 min

Tobie walks through a common administrative task: pulling a list of student IDs from one spreadsheet and finding the duplicates in another, so a faculty member can identify candidates for departmental awards. She uses Copilot (the free tier WPI provides) side-by-side on a second monitor — asking it how to highlight duplicates, trying the first prompt, catching that the wording didn't quite match what she needed, and refining until she gets the conditional-formatting rule she can apply.
She also uses Copilot for other Excel formulas and for email wording, and uses the free ChatGPT on her phone for personal projects like event flyers. The free tiers have been enough so far.

What you can try: Next time you hit a small Excel wall — a formula you half-remember, a conditional-formatting rule, a lookup across two sheets — open Copilot in a second window and describe the problem in plain language. If the first answer doesn't match what you actually need, refine the wording rather than giving up. Tobie's rule: "the wording definitely matters."

Making AI Use Explicit with the AI Assessment Scale and Paired Voice Memos

Richard Lopez
Assistant Professor, Social Science & Policy Studies
Faculty page →

Social Sciences Teaching 22 min

Richard's use of AI in the classroom is deliberately minimal and structured. He teaches psychology and neuroscience of digital media and AI, and wants students engaging directly with each other, not routing every thought through a chatbot. Two structural moves anchor his course: paper-based group exams (a method his colleague Dr. Angela Incollingo Rodriguez pioneered) and weekly Voice Memo Reflections (VMRs) — paired, at-least-ten-minute recorded conversations where students think through the week's material together.
This year he added the AI Assessment Scale (AIAS) to make AI use explicit for each part of the VMR assignment. Part 1 is individual prep, tagged AIAS Level 2 — AI is welcome for brainstorming and organizing notes. Part 2 is the actual paired recording: no AI at all, phone only for recording and keeping time. Every assignment also carries a short AI disclosure form where students report exactly how they used AI in Part 1.
By rotating pairings across VMRs and group exams, students end the term having genuinely worked with four or five different classmates. He notes that the interactions have taken off on their own: students overheard bragging that they "lost track of time" and went well past the ten-minute minimum.

What you can try: On your next assignment, state the AI Assessment Scale level right in the prompt (e.g., "AIAS Level 2: AI may be used for brainstorming and planning; final interpretations must be your own"), and add a short disclosure form where students describe how they actually used AI. Both students and instructor stop guessing about what's expected, and you get a running record of how AI is showing up in your class.

Generating Synthetic Case Studies and Datasets for a Marketing Analytics Class

Farnoush Reshadi
Assistant Professor, The Business School
Faculty page →

Business Teaching 13 min

Farnoush teaches marketing analytics in a case-study format using Python an uncommon combination for a marketing class and one that historically forced her to build cases and datasets from scratch. She describes spending two to three days on a single dataset, only to find (mid-lecture) a bug that took another semester to catch and fix.
In this walkthrough she shows how she used ChatGPT to generate a full teaching case in one prompt: a short business scenario plus a synthetic dataset she could use to teach data cleaning and visualization. She then refined it — asking the model to inject deliberate missing values and out-of-range entries so students would have something to clean, and to make the variables logically consistent.
She acknowledges this worked well for visualization, but she has not yet trusted it for the more complicated techniques she teaches (A/B testing, regression, neural networks, market-basket analysis) — and even synthetic data still needs a careful pass to make sure the numbers make sense.

What you can try: For your next data-cleaning or visualization lesson, ask AI to generate a small synthetic dataset with a short business (or discipline-specific) case study attached — and explicitly ask it to include a few missing values, out-of-range entries, and inconsistent columns for students to find. Reshadi got a usable first draft in a single prompt; iteration replaced what used to take days.

Modernizing CS Course Materials with AI: From Exercises to Interactive Demos

Dachun Sun
Assistant Teaching Professor, Computer Science
Faculty page →

Sciences Teaching Admin 20 min

Dachun walks through four ways he uses AI (mostly OpenAI's Codex on the $20 tier) across his Intro to AI, Intro to Programming, and Deep Learning courses.
He starts with concrete teaching analogies; asking AI for a metaphors or examples to better teach coding conventions. Then he shows a Word document lab inherited from a colleague being modernized as a WPI-themed HTML template that renders to PDF, styled from a PowerPoint screenshot he pasted into the chat. Next, a set of separate Gradescope autograders consolidated into one maintainable version with a build script that bundles per-assignment configurations. Finally, interactive lecture demos rebuilt from existing examples and bundled into a single self-contained HTML file that students open in any browser: no install, no server, and no dependencies.
Throughout, he is candid about the trade-off: AI writes the code, but you never read most of it; so the responsibility to actually test the output is on you.

What you can try: Next time you'd send students to a fragile online demo or ask them to install something, ask AI to bundle a small interactive demo (e.g. a slider, a chart, a simulation) into a single self-contained HTML file. Post it to Canvas; students open it in any browser. Works for any discipline, not just CS.

Designing a Business Course Around AI-Augmented Supply Chain Analysis

Sara Saberi
Associate Professor, The Business School
Faculty page →

Business Teaching 33 min

Sara walks through the first pass of a new graduate module she is designing for WPI's forthcoming MS in Supply Chain Management. The idea: teach future supply chain, procurement, and operations managers to work with AI on real inventory data rather than either avoiding it or offloading judgment to it.
Students first tackle a public Kaggle inventory dataset using only their own domain knowledge (reorder points, order quantities, lead times), then repeat the analysis with Claude Code guided by a claude.md file that gives the model a defined "expert consultant" persona.
She is candid that this is her first time teaching the course and that the material will change with each iteration. Her recurring theme: use AI, but own every sentence, every number, and every recommendation that comes out of it.

What you can try: Open the same prompt in two different AIs (for example, Claude and Gemini) and let each critique the other's suggestions. Saberi finds this surfaces angles neither model produces alone — useful for research responses, course design, or any decision where you want a second opinion before committing.

Building a Department MQP Dashboard with Plain-Language Prompts

Pradeep Radhakrishnan
Associate Professor of Teaching, Mechanical & Materials Engineering
Faculty page →

Engineering Teaching Admin 26 min

Pradeep demos a working dashboard he built for the Mechanical & Materials Engineering department to replace a scatter of spreadsheets for MQP tracking, budget management, and lab-access assignments. The stack is PHP + MySQL on his own web host, but he wrote none of it from scratch — Gemini generated the code, and he integrated and tested.
His starting prompt was short and non-technical: a plain-language description of the problem, plus a sample CSV from Qualtrics. Copilot kept steering him back toward Power BI (a Microsoft product) and a slow toolchain; Gemini proposed a lightweight web app and walked him through it step by step.
Both this dashboard and a companion lab-scheduling tool took roughly five hours each to build, test, and deploy. When errors appeared, he screenshotted them and pasted them straight into the chat — the model diagnosed most from the image alone.

What you can try: Next time you hit a technical wall — a permission error, a broken query, a stuck install — screenshot it and paste it into your AI chat before you Google the error message. Radhakrishnan built two production tools this way without writing SQL from memory.

Using AI Projects to Audit a Syllabus and Analyze a CV

Reza Ebadi
Assistant Professor of Teaching, Mechanical & Materials Engineering
Faculty page →

Engineering Teaching Admin 15 min

Reza walks through why he uses AI Projects (in Claude and ChatGPT) rather than one-off chats: persistent instructions, isolated memory, and threads organized by theme.
He demos two projects of his own. The first maps every objective in his ME3310 Kinematics syllabus to specific homework, quiz, and exam questions, surfacing lecture topics that are covered heavily but never actually assessed. The second ingests his CV to spot trends in course evaluations, organize MQP advising by theme, and draft speaker bios at three different lengths.
He also shares the "Claude Sandbox" folder convention he uses to give AI safe access to local files without exposing sensitive material.

What you can try: Create a new Project in Claude or ChatGPT, upload your CV, and ask it to summarize the trend in your course evaluations by course and flag anything worth a second look.
