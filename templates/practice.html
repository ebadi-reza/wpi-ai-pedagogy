Generating Synthetic Case Studies and Datasets for a Marketing Analytics Class

Farnoush Reshadi
Assistant Professor, The Business School
Faculty page →

Business Teaching 13 min

Farnoush teaches marketing analytics in a case-study format using Python an uncommon combination for a marketing class and one that historically forced her to build cases and datasets from scratch. She describes spending two to three days on a single dataset, only to find (mid-lecture) a bug that took another semester to catch and fix.
In this walkthrough she shows how she used ChatGPT to generate a full teaching case in one prompt: a short business scenario plus a synthetic dataset she could use to teach data cleaning and visualization. She then refined it — asking the model to inject deliberate missing values and out-of-range entries so students would have something to clean, and to make the variables logically consistent.
She acknowledges this worked well for visualization, but she has not yet trusted it for the more complicated techniques she teaches (A/B testing, regression, neural networks, market-basket analysis) — and even synthetic data still needs a careful pass to make sure the numbers make sense.

What you can try: For your next data-cleaning or visualization lesson, ask AI to generate a small synthetic dataset with a short business (or discipline-specific) case study attached — and explicitly ask it to include a few missing values, out-of-range entries, and inconsistent columns for students to find. Reshadi got a usable first draft in a single prompt; iteration replaced what used to take days.

Modernizing CS Course Materials with AI: From Exercises to Interactive Demos

Dachun Sun
Assistant Teaching Professor, Computer Science
Faculty page →

Sciences Teaching Admin 20 min

Dachun walks through four ways he uses AI (mostly OpenAI's Codex on the $20 tier) across his Intro to AI, Intro to Programming, and Deep Learning courses.
He starts with concrete teaching analogies; asking AI for a metaphors or examples to better teach coding conventions. Then he shows a Word document lab inherited from a colleague being modernized as a WPI-themed HTML template that renders to PDF, styled from a PowerPoint screenshot he pasted into the chat. Next, a set of separate Gradescope autograders consolidated into one maintainable version with a build script that bundles per-assignment configurations. Finally, interactive lecture demos rebuilt from existing examples and bundled into a single self-contained HTML file that students open in any browser: no install, no server, and no dependencies.
Throughout, he is candid about the trade-off: AI writes the code, but you never read most of it; so the responsibility to actually test the output is on you.

What you can try: Next time you'd send students to a fragile online demo or ask them to install something, ask AI to bundle a small interactive demo (e.g. a slider, a chart, a simulation) into a single self-contained HTML file. Post it to Canvas; students open it in any browser. Works for any discipline, not just CS.

Designing a Business Course Around AI-Augmented Supply Chain Analysis

Sara Saberi
Associate Professor, The Business School
Faculty page →

Business Teaching 33 min

Sara walks through the first pass of a new graduate module she is designing for WPI's forthcoming MS in Supply Chain Management. The idea: teach future supply chain, procurement, and operations managers to work with AI on real inventory data rather than either avoiding it or offloading judgment to it.
Students first tackle a public Kaggle inventory dataset using only their own domain knowledge (reorder points, order quantities, lead times), then repeat the analysis with Claude Code guided by a claude.md file that gives the model a defined "expert consultant" persona.
She is candid that this is her first time teaching the course and that the material will change with each iteration. Her recurring theme: use AI, but own every sentence, every number, and every recommendation that comes out of it.

What you can try: Open the same prompt in two different AIs (for example, Claude and Gemini) and let each critique the other's suggestions. Saberi finds this surfaces angles neither model produces alone — useful for research responses, course design, or any decision where you want a second opinion before committing.

Building a Department MQP Dashboard with Plain-Language Prompts

Pradeep Radhakrishnan
Associate Professor of Teaching, Mechanical & Materials Engineering
Faculty page →

Engineering Teaching Admin 26 min

Pradeep demos a working dashboard he built for the Mechanical & Materials Engineering department to replace a scatter of spreadsheets for MQP tracking, budget management, and lab-access assignments. The stack is PHP + MySQL on his own web host, but he wrote none of it from scratch — Gemini generated the code, and he integrated and tested.
His starting prompt was short and non-technical: a plain-language description of the problem, plus a sample CSV from Qualtrics. Copilot kept steering him back toward Power BI (a Microsoft product) and a slow toolchain; Gemini proposed a lightweight web app and walked him through it step by step.
Both this dashboard and a companion lab-scheduling tool took roughly five hours each to build, test, and deploy. When errors appeared, he screenshotted them and pasted them straight into the chat — the model diagnosed most from the image alone.

What you can try: Next time you hit a technical wall — a permission error, a broken query, a stuck install — screenshot it and paste it into your AI chat before you Google the error message. Radhakrishnan built two production tools this way without writing SQL from memory.

Using AI Projects to Audit a Syllabus and Analyze a CV

Reza Ebadi
Assistant Professor of Teaching, Mechanical & Materials Engineering
Faculty page →

Engineering Teaching Admin 15 min

Reza walks through why he uses AI Projects (in Claude and ChatGPT) rather than one-off chats: persistent instructions, isolated memory, and threads organized by theme.
He demos two projects of his own. The first maps every objective in his ME3310 Kinematics syllabus to specific homework, quiz, and exam questions, surfacing lecture topics that are covered heavily but never actually assessed. The second ingests his CV to spot trends in course evaluations, organize MQP advising by theme, and draft speaker bios at three different lengths.
He also shares the "Claude Sandbox" folder convention he uses to give AI safe access to local files without exposing sensitive material.

What you can try: Create a new Project in Claude or ChatGPT, upload your CV, and ask it to summarize the trend in your course evaluations by course and flag anything worth a second look.
